# What plays teach

Method notes for studying plays the writer shares. Study how, never copy wording, characters or plot. Only list a play here as read if it was read. Keep unpublished scripts, and notes that identify them, out of anything you share.

## Measure first
`read_play.py` prints turn length, sentence length and direction words per spoken word. Compare three things: a play the writer admires, the writer's own draft, and the current revision. Scanned text gives rough counts. They're a mirror, not a target.

| | average words per turn | turns of 8 words or fewer | turns of 20+ | directions per spoken word |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *The Piano Lesson*, August Wilson (rough, from a scan) | 32 | 23% | 44% | 0.08 |
| A one-act whose writer called it choppy | 7 | 72% | 5% | 0.55 |
| The same one-act, rewritten | 12 | 54% | 17% | 0.25 |

Short lines aren't the fault by themselves. Some plays are written in very short lines and sound natural, because people finish, overlap and correct each other instead of scoring.

## Dialogue that isn't choppy
- **Join the thoughts.** People say the thing, then why, then a detail, then what they want: "and", "so", "because", "but".
- **Wrap the big thing in ordinary talk.** Let the want ride under something practical: shoes, a bus, a form to fill in.
- **Ask again.** Someone who doesn't get an answer asks the same question a little differently, or asks someone else.
- **Stay on the subject.** Pick up the other person's noun and keep it going before changing.
- **Let an answer run past the question.** A fact, a name, a small complaint. Then stop.
- **Save the volley.** Short lines belong where somebody is cornered. If the whole scene is short, the corner doesn't register.
- **Long is not the same as facing front.** Every long speech still asks this listener for something. If the listener could leave and the speech wouldn't change, cut it.

## Setting and directions
- **Set it once.** One paragraph at the top: when, where, the light, who is there and what they're in the middle of. Then start talking.
- **Few and physical.** After that: entrances, exits, a thing handed over, one touch. Nothing about how to say a line.
- **Almost no beats.** Let a change of subject make the pause. Keep "(Beat.)" for a silence that changes something.
- **Two or three places, always the same.** Name the place in a word and let light and sound do the rest. Move between scenes with the same actors and the same object.
- **An object carries the story** because people handle it, pass it and quarrel over it.
