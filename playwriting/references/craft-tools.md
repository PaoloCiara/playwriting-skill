# Craft tools

Working notes on the vocabulary this skill uses, drawn mainly from Stuart Spencer's *The Playwright's Guidebook* and restated for use at the desk. Chapter names are given so the writer can go to the book, which argues and illustrates all of this far more fully.

Contents: Want · Why and what's underneath · What's in the way · Stakes and hope · What changes · Beats and scenes · Character · Plot · Suspense · Exposition · Direct address

## Want (ch. Action)
- Dramatic action is an inner need, not movement and not emotion. A busy stage or a weeping one can still have none.
- Ask it of characters, never of "the play". Everyone onstage has one, even briefly.
- It must show up in what is said or done, and be aimed at someone present who could satisfy it.
- **Concrete beats abstract.** Give the need an object. A want you can hold gives actors something to play and stops characters from restating their need.
- Test: ask a reader "what does X want here?" without hinting. A wrong or blank answer is the note.

## Why and what's underneath (ch. Motivation and Subtext)
- What they want and why they want it are separate choices; the same want with a new why makes a new play.
- Stack it: surface want, hidden want, deepest want. Each should lead to the next.
- A perfectly clear want, fully known from the start, leaves nothing to discover.
- The quiet motive is usually better than the sensational one.

## What's in the way (ch. Conflict)
- Conflict is the obstacle, not the volume. Two people being kind can be in fierce conflict.
- Direct: both want control of one thing. Indirect: A wants something from B, while B wants something else. Often the second is richer.
- Internal: two wants in one person. Nobody can play both at once, so alternate them by beat.
- Argument is disagreement about a subject. It can sit inside conflict but can't replace it.
- If both want the same outcome, look for who can't yet afford it.

## Stakes and hope (ch. High Stakes and High Hopes)
- Stakes: what's gained or lost. Raise them by tying the people closer, not by adding misery.
- Prefer people who already matter to each other.
- Hope: the goal must stay possible in the character's mind. An obstacle with no crack in it kills interest.

## What changes (ch. Event)
- The wanting ends: they get it, they clearly don't, or something larger happens because they tried. The third kind usually resonates most.
- The change is a decision or a recognition. What we see is its shock wave, so write the visible thing that follows.
- It must come from the struggle. Rescue by luck, news or a stranger is arbitrary, however neat.

## Beats and scenes (ch. Beats, Scenes, Acts)
- A beat is the smallest unit with want, obstacle and small change; in practice, one tactic. New tactic, new beat.
- A scene is a run of beats that ends in a change big enough to alter the want.
- Pressure may dip after a change but should trend upward.

## Character (ch. Character)
- Traits lean toward feelings, feelings toward wants, wants into speech and behaviour. If a character is foggy, step back one link.
- Quirks are decoration.
- Understandable matters more than likeable. Give the hard ones their reasons.
- A passive lead is often an over-managed one. Check each of their lines for a want.

## Language (ch. Character, "Language", pp. 151–153)
- Dialogue isn't a separate layer to polish. Think less about how lines sound and more about what state the speaker is in and what they're doing with the words.
- Method: read the scene for each person's main emotion. Mark, without changing, the lines and words that don't come from it. Then ask of each: is the emotion not here yet? could the scene start later? did the draft miss the real impulse? is the person hiding it (then let it leak), or unaware of it? "They're hiding it" is also the easy excuse.
- Emotion gives rise to action, so a wrong-sounding line may mean the want is wrong.
- A voice is found by writing honestly, not made. It belongs to the characters: let them speak their way, not the writer's.
- Put physical action in the words ("This lid won't turn") so an actor has to do it.
- A list of quirks isn't a character.

## Plot (ch. The Journey of the Play)
- Story is everything that happened. Plot is what we watch, chosen for cause and effect.
- Test order: if two scenes swap without damage, the link is missing.
- Out-of-sequence plays still need causation, through meaning: each scene must change how we read the last.
- Within a scene, listening is causation.
- Choose an exact gap between scenes. Hours leave people raw; weeks let them harden.

## Suspense
- Tell more, not less, then make them wait.
- Or keep them asking what someone is really after.

## Exposition (ch. Exposition)
- Audiences want information; they resist being handed it. Ration it.
- Let a need push it out. If the one who knows won't tell, send someone in to dig.
- If it won't come out naturally, it may not belong in the play. The writer may need it; the audience may not.
- Ambiguity is allowed when nobody would wonder.
- A line that states the play's theme is cut if the play already shows it, kept if a character needs it.

## Direct address (ch. Exposition)
- Seamless speech: to another character, still driven by a want.
- Soliloquy: alone, working out a real dilemma that moves.
- Address to the house: strongest when the audience is cast as someone, or openly itself, with a rule set early.
- Weakest: someone musing aloud with no one to reach and no problem to solve.

## Revising (ch. Rewriting)
- Leave alone what works.
- Question the givens that feel fixed.
- Decide what this draft is for, then hold to it.
- Many different complaints about one spot mean the spot is wrong, even if every suggested fix is wrong too.
